\section*{Introduction}

In recent years, criminals have increasingly resorted to outdated devices and technologies to evade detection. 2G handsets, in particular, serve as effective anti-forensics tools due to their minimal features, which limit digital footprints compared to modern smartphones. This trend of reusing old technology to remain ``invisible'' is further explored in articles such as ``The Analog Way to Stay Invisible.''

In Kenya, these so-called 
\emph{Mulika Mwizi} phones (cheap feature phones like Nokia classics or Itel models) are commonly used in criminal activities, especially phone-snatching rings and fraud schemes. Their appeal lies in being inexpensive, purchasable with cash, and easily disposable---reducing traceable evidence.

Admissibility challenges for evidence from such old devices are growing, often at significant cost to prosecutions. Criminals treat 2G handsets as single-use items to avoid leaving digital trails. Internationally, similar tactics appear, such as the reuse of pagers for communication in parts of the Middle East.

Recently, Kenya's Directorate of Criminal Investigations (DCI) has recovered hundreds of suspected stolen devices in operations targeting black-market repair shops and theft networks (e.g., March 2024 seizures in Nairobi regions).

A DCI cybercrime expert has emphasized that 
\emph{Mulika Mwizi} phones are traceable via carrier data (e.g., call and SMS logs, IMSI/IMEI records), debunking the myth that they cannot be tracked.

However, physical extraction from the old hardware itself poses serious integrity challenges, frequently leading to inadmissibility in court. Even carrier-based traceability (IMSI/IMEI logs) can fail integrity checks if not properly documented.

\section*{Are Courts and Law Enforcement Authorities Prepared?}

Concerns persist about the reliability of digital forensic evidence in Kenyan courts. A key issue is the admissibility of evidence from digitally investigated cases.

Evidence must be obtained using scientifically reliable, consistent, and integrity-preserving methods and standards. As a result, many digital forensic cases fail to proceed to trial and are often settled out of court.

Another challenge is the limited understanding of digital forensics among judges, lawyers, and juries. Digital (or computer) forensics is a relatively new discipline, emerging from rapid advancements in computing, mobile phones, and handheld devices---from traditional PDAs to modern smartphones and cellular PDAs.

\subsection*{Traditional Legal Reference: Daubert Criteria}

The Daubert standard, originating from the 1993 U.S.
case \emph{Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals}, serves as a foundational reference for admissibility of scientific evidence, including digital forensics. Courts worldwide, including those in common-law jurisdictions like Kenya, draw on its principles when developing procedures.

Meyers and Rogers (2004) note that Daubert applies to science, technology, and engineering fields. Key factors include testability, peer review, error rates, and general acceptance in the relevant scientific community. Experts often face frailties in meeting these---particularly error rates, peer review, and acceptability---when tools or methods lack validation.

\subsection*{Australian Evidence Act}

For comparison, Australia's \emph{Evidence Act 1995} (formerly 1924 principles in Pt VIA) addresses computer output reliability. Section 59B stipulates conditions such as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item No alterations to the computer's mechanisms or processes during the material time that could reasonably affect output accuracy.
  \item No reasonable belief that accuracy was adversely affected by improper processes, procedures, or inadequate safeguards.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Kenya Evidence Act Cap 80}

Sections 106A--106I govern the admissibility of electronic or digital records in Kenya. Section 106B requires a certificate of authenticity to prove integrity, detailing conditions like proper device operation, no material alterations, and reliable safeguards. Courts demand this certification, which poses a major hurdle for extractions from old 2G devices where tools are outdated and chain-of-custody is fragile.

In practice, many forensic tools fail to satisfy Daubert-inspired reliability tests, Kenya's Evidence Act requirements, and Australian-like safeguards.

Legacy tools such as Palm dd, Pilot-link, POSE, PDA Seizure, EnCase, and Duplicate Disk remain relevant for some investigations but are outdated. Modern, court-validated tools like Cellebrite, Oxygen Forensics, and Magnet AXIOM are recommended for better reliability.

2G 
\emph{Mulika Mwizi} phones present unique challenges: they yield minimal data (call logs, SMS, no app logs), reducing the investigative surface. Proprietary file formats complicate extraction and processing, and many older models lack JTAG/ISP support. Their disposability breaks chain of custody, while outdated forensic tools lacking vendor support produce evidence with higher error rates that fail modern integrity standards.

\section*{Conclusion}

Although carrier data provides traceability (e.g., logs via IMSI/IMEI), hardware extraction integrity issues often render evidence inadmissible. Law enforcement and courts must adopt validated modern tools, rigorous certification processes, and updated training to counter this analog resurgence and ensure successful prosecutions.

\section*{Sample Research Methodology for Future Work}

Future studies could design controlled lab experiments to quantify these challenges. A proposed research project could focus on a Symbian-based cellular PDA, such as the Nokia E61 or the 2700 series. Using quantitative methods, the study would address the following objectives:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Primary Objective:} Conduct acquisition experiments with available tools to measure and observe error rates. Error rate is treated as a measurable variable derived from hypothesis testing ($H_0$ refutable, $H_1$ acceptable). It serves as a superset ($E$) encompassing sub-variables: credibility ($C$), clarity ($C_1$), acceptability ($A$), and testability ($T$).
  \item \textbf{Secondary Objective:} Apply statistical methods such as discriminant analysis to categorize tool performance and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess significance and variance in results.
  \item \textbf{Tertiary Objective:} Develop a replicable model from the process to support future experiments and validate findings against standards like Daubert criteria.
\end{itemize}

This structured approach could empirically demonstrate admissibility gaps and guide tool validation in Kenyan contexts.
